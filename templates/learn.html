Concept First
Imagine you want to know where a stock price might be in one year. You can't know for sure — but you can simulate thousands of possible futures based on how volatile the stock has been historically. Each simulated path uses a random daily return drawn from a normal distribution matching the stock's past behavior. Run 500 paths and you get a probability distribution of outcomes.
Monte Carlo Simulation
P(t+1) = P(t) × (1 + N(μ, σ)) where μ = historical mean daily return, σ = historical daily std dev Run N simulations, collect final prices → build probability distribution
Used to estimate Value at Risk, stress-test portfolios, price options, and understand the range of possible outcomes before entering a trade.

Concept First
A strategy that earns 30% a year sounds great — until you learn it required a 50% drawdown to get there. Risk-adjusted return metrics let you compare strategies fairly by normalizing returns against the risk taken to achieve them.
Sharpe Ratio
Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) / σp Rp = portfolio return, Rf = risk-free rate (e.g. 5%), σp = std dev of returns Annualize: multiply mean daily return × 252, std × √252
A Sharpe above 1.0 is acceptable. Above 2.0 is strong. Below 0 means you'd do better in T-bills.
Concept First
Sharpe ratio penalizes both upside and downside volatility equally. But investors only care about downside risk. Sortino fixes this by only using the standard deviation of negative returns (downside deviation) in the denominator.
Sortino Ratio
Sortino = (Rp − Rf) / σ_downside σ_downside = std dev of returns BELOW the target/threshold (usually 0 or Rf) Higher is better — a Sortino of 2+ is strong.
Preferred by many hedge funds. A strategy can have a low Sharpe but high Sortino if its volatility is mostly upside — that's a good thing.
Concept First
Before you can risk losing money, you need to know the most you could lose. Maximum Drawdown measures the peak-to-trough decline of a portfolio. If your $100k account drops to $65k before recovering, the max drawdown is 35%.
Maximum Drawdown (MDD)
MDD = (Trough Value − Peak Value) / Peak Value × 100 Calmar Ratio = Annualized Return / |Max Drawdown| A Calmar above 1.0 means you earn more than you risk losing in a year.
Funded trader firms (TopStep, FTMO) use max drawdown as the primary evaluation metric. Breaching it fails your account.
Concept First
If I told you there's a 95% chance you won't lose more than $2,000 tomorrow, that's VaR. It doesn't tell you how bad things get in the worst 5% — that's CVaR/Expected Shortfall — but it gives a practical daily risk budget.
Value at Risk (VaR)
Parametric VaR = Portfolio × σ × Z Z-scores: 90% = 1.282 | 95% = 1.645 | 99% = 2.326 Example: $50k × 1.5% daily σ × 1.645 = $1,234 daily 95% VaR
Banks and prop firms use VaR to set position limits. If your daily VaR exceeds a threshold, risk management forces you to reduce exposure.
Concept First
Imagine you flip a coin that pays $2 on heads, loses $1 on tails. You have a 50% win rate. How much of your bankroll should you bet each flip to grow fastest without risking ruin? Kelly answers this exactly. Too much and variance destroys you; too little leaves money on the table.
Kelly Criterion
f* = (b × p − q) / b b = net odds (win/loss ratio), p = win probability, q = 1 − p Simplified for trading: f* = (Win Rate × Avg Win − Loss Rate × Avg Loss) / Avg Win Most practitioners use Half-Kelly (f*/2) to reduce variance
Maximizes long-run geometric growth rate. Critical for position sizing — tells you exactly what fraction of capital to risk per trade.
Concept First
Fixed fractional position sizing says: risk the same percentage of your account on every trade regardless of setup quality. If you risk 1% per trade on a $50k account, you risk $500 per trade. Your position size then depends on where you put your stop-loss.
Fixed Fractional Position Sizing
Risk per trade = Account × Risk % Shares = Risk per trade / (Entry Price − Stop Loss Price) Example: $50k × 1% = $500 risk. Entry $100, Stop $95 → $500/$5 = 100 shares
The most widely used position sizing method. Scales naturally as your account grows or shrinks — a losing streak automatically reduces size.
Concept First
A reward:risk ratio tells you how much you stand to gain relative to how much you risk. If you risk $100 to make $300, your R:R is 3:1. Even with a 40% win rate, a 3:1 R:R strategy is profitable in the long run.
Expected Value & R:R Ratio
EV = (Win Rate × Avg Win) − (Loss Rate × Avg Loss) Minimum win rate for breakeven = 1 / (1 + R:R) At 2:1 R:R → need only 33.3% win rate to break even
Many professional traders focus entirely on R:R ratios. A 30% win rate with 5:1 R:R is more profitable than 60% win rate with 1:1.
Concept First
Prices don't drift randomly forever — they tend to pull back toward an average over time. A rubber band stretches but snaps back. When a stock's price deviates significantly from its historical mean (measured by Z-score), you bet on it returning. This is the foundation of statistical arbitrage.
Mean Reversion
Z-score = (Current Price − Moving Average) / Std Dev of Price Entry signal: |Z| > 2.0 (price is 2 standard deviations from mean) Exit signal: Z approaches 0 (price returns to mean)
Most effective in range-bound markets. Fails when a trend breaks structure. Works well with pairs of correlated assets (pairs trading).
Concept First
Stocks that have outperformed recently tend to keep outperforming over the next 3–12 months. This is momentum. The opposite of mean reversion. It works because institutional investors move slowly — they can't buy their full position at once, so a stock rises gradually as they accumulate.
Momentum / Trend Following
Rate of Change (ROC) = (P_now − P_n_periods_ago) / P_n_periods_ago × 100 Cross-sectional momentum: rank all assets by 12-1 month return, buy top decile Time-series momentum: buy if 12-month return > 0, sell if < 0
One of the most robust anomalies in finance, documented across stocks, bonds, commodities, and currencies. Works in trending markets; whipsaws in choppy ones.
Concept First
Beta measures how much a stock moves relative to the market. A beta of 1.5 means the stock moves 1.5% for every 1% market move. Alpha is the return above what beta predicts — it's the "skill" portion. CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model) formalizes the relationship between risk and expected return.
Alpha, Beta & CAPM
Expected Return = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf) Alpha = Actual Return − Expected Return (CAPM) Beta = Cov(stock, market) / Var(market) Positive alpha = outperforming on a risk-adjusted basis
Every fund manager is judged on alpha. Beta is "free" risk from market exposure — you want to be paid for beta, but alpha is what separates skill from luck.
Concept First
Two stocks that move together aren't just correlated — they're cointegrated if their prices are linked by a long-run equilibrium. Even if they drift apart short term, they always come back together. Pairs trading exploits this: short the outperformer, long the underperformer, wait for convergence.
Pairs Trading & Cointegration
Spread = Price_A − hedge_ratio × Price_B hedge_ratio = from OLS regression of A on B Entry: spread Z-score > 2 → short spread (sell A, buy B) Exit: Z-score returns to 0
Market-neutral strategy — profit doesn't depend on market direction. Commonly used with ETF pairs (SPY/QQQ), sector peers (AAPL/MSFT), or commodity futures.
Concept First
An option gives you the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a set price (strike) before expiry. The price of that option depends on five things: stock price, strike, time to expiry, volatility, and interest rates. Black-Scholes gives the exact formula.
Black-Scholes Option Pricing
C = S × N(d1) − K × e^(−rT) × N(d2) d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T) d2 = d1 − σ√T S=stock, K=strike, r=risk-free rate, T=time(years), σ=volatility, N()=normal CDF
The foundation of options pricing. Understanding it helps you identify mispriced options and design hedging strategies.
Concept First
The Greeks measure how sensitive an option's price is to each input. They're your risk dashboard: Delta tells you directional exposure, Gamma tells you how fast Delta changes, Theta bleeds value every day, Vega profits from volatility spikes, Rho responds to interest rates.
The Options Greeks (Δ Γ Θ V ρ)
Delta (Δ): ΔOption / ΔStock — range 0 to 1 for calls, -1 to 0 for puts Gamma (Γ): ΔDelta / ΔStock — highest for at-the-money near expiry Theta (Θ): ΔOption / Δtime — always negative, time decay accelerates near expiry Vega (V): ΔOption / Δvolatility — long options = long Vega Rho (ρ): ΔOption / Δinterest rate — minor for short-dated options
Market makers delta-hedge continuously to stay directionally neutral. Retail traders use Greeks to manage risk and find asymmetric setups.
Concept First
Implied Volatility (IV) is what the market thinks future volatility will be, extracted backward from the current option price. When IV is high, options are expensive. When IV is low, options are cheap. The VIX is simply the 30-day IV of the S&P 500.
Implied Volatility & IV Rank
IV Rank = (Current IV − 52-week low IV) / (52-week high IV − 52-week low IV) × 100 IV Percentile: what % of days in past year had lower IV than today IV Rank > 50 → sell premium (options expensive) IV Rank < 30 → buy options (options cheap)
Professional options traders don't trade direction — they trade volatility. Selling premium when IV is high and buying when low is the core of many options strategies.
Concept First
You have two risky assets. If they move together perfectly, combining them doesn't help. But if they move oppositely (correlation = -1), combining them eliminates all risk. The Efficient Frontier is the set of all portfolios that maximize return for a given level of risk. Every optimal portfolio lies on it.
Modern Portfolio Theory & Efficient Frontier
Portfolio Return = w1×R1 + w2×R2 + ... Portfolio Variance = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2w1w2σ1σ2ρ12 Correlation ρ12 = Cov(A,B) / (σA × σB) Optimal portfolio = highest Sharpe ratio on the frontier
The mathematical proof that diversification reduces risk without sacrificing expected return. The basis of all modern index fund construction.
Concept First
Correlation tells you how two assets move together on a scale of -1 to +1. But correlation is not constant — it spikes toward 1 during market crashes (everything falls together). This is correlation breakdown, and it's why diversification sometimes fails exactly when you need it most.
Correlation & Covariance
Covariance = E[(Ri − μi)(Rj − μj)] Correlation ρ = Cov(i,j) / (σi × σj) ρ = +1: perfect positive | ρ = 0: no relationship | ρ = -1: perfect inverse Low/negative correlation between assets → diversification benefit
Portfolio construction always starts with correlations. Assets with low correlation reduce portfolio volatility even if their individual risk is high.
Concept First
Factor models decompose a stock's return into exposure to systematic factors: market, size, value, profitability, momentum, etc. The Fama-French 3-Factor model showed that small-cap stocks and value stocks outperform long-term because they carry extra risk that CAPM doesn't capture.
Factor Models (Fama-French)
Ri − Rf = α + β_mkt(Rm−Rf) + β_smb(SMB) + β_hml(HML) + ε SMB = Small Minus Big (size factor) HML = High Minus Low (value factor) 5-Factor adds: RMW (profitability) + CMA (investment)
Every quant fund uses factor models to decompose returns and identify whether alpha is real or just hidden factor exposure. "Smart beta" ETFs are essentially factor portfolios.
Concept First
A moving average smooths out price noise by averaging over a rolling window. Simple Moving Average (SMA) weights all days equally. Exponential Moving Average (EMA) weights recent prices more heavily. When a short-period EMA crosses above a long-period EMA, it's a bullish signal.
Moving Averages (SMA / EMA)
SMA(n) = (P1 + P2 + ... + Pn) / n EMA(t) = Price(t) × k + EMA(t-1) × (1−k) k = 2 / (n + 1) — smoothing factor Golden Cross: 50-day SMA crosses above 200-day SMA → bullish Death Cross: 50-day SMA crosses below 200-day SMA → bearish
The most widely used technical indicator. Used for trend identification, support/resistance levels, and crossover signals.
Concept First
Bollinger Bands place two bands 2 standard deviations above and below a 20-day SMA. When price touches the upper band, it's statistically overbought. When it touches the lower band, it's oversold. Band squeeze (bands narrowing) signals a breakout is coming.
Bollinger Bands
Middle Band = SMA(20) Upper Band = SMA(20) + 2 × σ(20) Lower Band = SMA(20) − 2 × σ(20) %B = (Price − Lower Band) / (Upper Band − Lower Band) Bandwidth = (Upper − Lower) / Middle
Combines trend-following (middle band) with volatility (band width). %B above 1.0 = price broke above upper band; below 0 = broke below lower band.
Concept First
RSI measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes. It compares average gains vs average losses over 14 periods. The result is a 0-100 oscillator. Above 70 = overbought (potential sell). Below 30 = oversold (potential buy). Divergence is more powerful: price makes new high but RSI doesn't → weakening momentum.
RSI — Relative Strength Index
RS = Avg Gain(14) / Avg Loss(14) RSI = 100 − (100 / (1 + RS)) Overbought: RSI > 70 | Oversold: RSI < 30 Bullish divergence: price makes lower low, RSI makes higher low Bearish divergence: price makes higher high, RSI makes lower high
Most effective at identifying reversals in ranging markets. In strong trends, RSI can stay overbought/oversold for extended periods.
Concept First
MACD is the difference between a 12-period EMA and a 26-period EMA. The Signal Line is a 9-period EMA of MACD. The histogram shows the gap between them. When MACD crosses above the Signal Line, it's bullish. The histogram turning from negative to positive is an early buy signal.
MACD — Moving Average Convergence Divergence
MACD Line = EMA(12) − EMA(26) Signal Line = EMA(9) of MACD Line Histogram = MACD Line − Signal Line Bullish crossover: MACD > Signal Line Zero-line cross: MACD crossing 0 confirms trend change
Combines trend direction (EMA crossovers) with momentum (histogram). Best used on daily/weekly charts. Less reliable on very short timeframes.
Head & Shoulders
Bearish reversal: three peaks — left shoulder, higher head, right shoulder at same level as left. Break of neckline (through the troughs) confirms pattern. Target = head height projected below neckline.
Inverse H&S
Bullish reversal: mirror image of H&S. Three troughs with the middle (head) lowest. Break above neckline on high volume confirms. Common bottoming pattern after a downtrend.
Bull Flag
Continuation pattern: sharp rally (flagpole) followed by a slight pullback in a channel (flag). Breakout above upper channel line with volume surge = entry signal. Target = flagpole length added to breakout point.
Bear Flag
Sharp selloff (pole) followed by a slight bounce in an upward channel. Break below lower channel line on volume = short entry. Target = pole length subtracted from breakdown point.
Ascending Triangle
Bullish consolidation: flat upper resistance + rising lower trendline. Buyers making higher lows shows increasing demand. Breakout above resistance usually explosive. Target = triangle height added to breakout level.
Descending Triangle
Bearish consolidation: flat support + falling upper trendline. Sellers making lower highs. Break below support on volume = short signal. Mirror image of ascending triangle.
Cup & Handle
Bullish continuation: rounded bottom (cup) followed by a small pullback (handle). Handle should be less than 50% of cup depth. Breakout above cup rim is buy signal. Used by William O'Neil's CAN SLIM method.
Double Bottom / Top
Double Bottom (W): price tests same support twice, rallies sharply. Double Top (M): price tests same resistance twice, breaks down. Confirmation when price breaks the middle peak/trough (neckline).
Fibonacci Retracements
After a move, price often retraces to Fibonacci levels: 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, 78.6%. The 61.8% level (golden ratio) is the strongest. Traders watch these levels for bounce entries during a pullback in a trend.
Wedge Patterns
Rising Wedge: both trendlines slope up but converge → bearish reversal. Falling Wedge: both slope down and converge → bullish reversal. Breakout direction is opposite to wedge direction — counterintuitive but reliable.
VWAP
Volume-Weighted Average Price = cumulative (price × volume) / cumulative volume. Intraday benchmark: price above VWAP = bullish bias; below = bearish. Institutional orders use VWAP to minimize market impact.
Support & Resistance
Support: price level where buyers historically step in. Resistance: where sellers historically overwhelm buyers. When broken, they swap roles (support becomes resistance, and vice versa). The more times a level is tested, the more significant the eventual break.
